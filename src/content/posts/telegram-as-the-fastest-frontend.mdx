i consider my self a backend person, so for me it's important to test the functionality of my ideas first. this situation made to consider using telegram for inputs/outputs.

telegram is free! so it's cheap in decisions. spinning a bot is faster and easier to spin my hetzner VPS using coolify to connect my backend to frontend.
notifications, voice notes, photos, buttons, and a phone-shaped UI come with
it. i usually don't build any of that before I know whether the idea is worth keeping.

## The blank project

Open an empty web project and the side quests show up before the feature does.
Auth? should i test on a phone? Where does state live? asking AI to edit the buttons or UI? What
sends notifications, and which screen do they open? Suddenly it's 3am at night
and I'm sleepy with a UI layout for a tool with one user (myself)!

that work isn't wasted. it's just expensive while I still don't know if the
core loop deserves to exist. For most of my tools, that first loop is tiny.

## KinoWeek

it was christmas time, i was on holidays and i wanted to check the English language movies or live musics in Hannover without checking too many websites, with their cookies and shitty sitemaps and filterings. i was like I don't need an account, a dashboard, or a settings page. I just wan one message a week with the good stuff already
filtered.

so that's what i built. it collected the events, formatted them, and sent the digest to Telegram. I opened the app I was already in, scanned the week, moved on.

Then it outgrew the chat. Once the question shifted from "what's my week" to
"what's goin on in this city," a real site made sense, and KinoWeek became BoringHannover. Browsing, filtering, sharing links, showing up in search. The web is good at that.

The workflow told me when it needed a frontend. I didn't have to guess on day one. (link of github & website here)

## FabrikTakt

i have been working in industry and manufacturing almost all of my life 
Different day & idea: capture practical manufacturing knowledge as text and voice,
including Persian voice notes, and make it searchable.

The hard part was getting anyone to capture anything at all. Telegram was the
obvious call. Everyone already had it and knew how to hold a button and talk.
Nothing to install, nothing to explain. Someone could describe a machine issue
the way they'd describe it to a colleague.

We look at a chat box and see limits. The person using it sees something they
already know how to work. That counts for a lot when the alternative feels like
paperwork.

## Hermes and OpenClaw

Hermes was a throwaway experiment. Watch a small stock-monitoring workflow, ping
me when something interesting showed up. Telegram was faster to set up than
WhatsApp, so I wired the alert, ran it, and found out quickly whether I cared.
If the answer had been no, I'd have deleted a bot and a script instead of
maintaining a frontend for an idea I'd lost interest in.

I still run this pattern. OpenClaw gives me one chat for a few narrow jobs.
Every now and then I ask how my Hetzner box is doing and it reports back disk,
memory, uptime, service health. Read-only, on purpose. I get to ask from my
phone, with boring guardrails behind it.

## When a bot is the wrong call

I wouldn't ship everything this way. Chat gets clumsy when the job is comparing
a lot of things, digging through history, managing permissions, or laying
information out visually. It's also a bad home for pages that should be public
and searchable.

BoringHannover is the clean example. A weekly digest was great in chat. A city
guide needed the web. So my rule of thumb comes down to the verbs.

There's no prize for starting with the fancier stack.

So if you're staring at an empty React project for a weekend tool, give it ten
minutes first. Write down the one message it should send you, or the one thing
you want to send it. Build that. Live with it for a week.

If you end up wishing for filters, history, and a real screen, good. That's the
workflow telling you what to build next.
